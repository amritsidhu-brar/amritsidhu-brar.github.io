Linguistic prescriptivism and aesthetics

Amrit Sidhu-Brar
January 2026
Originally published on The Heart-Shaped Grape
Linguistic prescriptivism is the idea that some linguistic usages (e.g. words, grammar, dialects etc.) are correct/preferred, and others are wrong. If you ever hear one native speaker telling another that what they said was “incorrect grammar”, that’s prescriptivism.
This section just explains linguistic prescriptivism and why it doesn’t make much sense as it’s usually couched. Nothing particularly unconventional here.
People who study linguistics tend to roundly reject prescriptivism. The reason is that there’s just no good/objective basis for determining which usages are “correct”.
There are a few, explicit or implicit, sources of legitimacy/correctness that prescriptivism (implicitly or explicitly) claims:
  • Conservatism: i.e. an implicit claim that language as it existed in the past was correct, but nowadays people are speaking differently and that’s bad.
    • Often with some implication that this is modern people being lazy, uneducated, classless; or bemoaning some foreign influence.
    • But this doesn’t make sense because there’s no logical stopping point. No-one is suggesting that all English speakers should speak like Chaucer, the Beowulf poet, or indeed should speak Proto-Indo-European. So it’s ultimately a value judgement too.
  • Reform: our language is messy/bad/vulgar/inconsistent, not like those respectable languages. We need to reform it to be better.
    • But this is obviously just a direct value judgement that some language varieties are better than others. There’s no basis for this that I think makes sense. Latin and English are different, but any way in which one is seen as better than the other is just a cultural judgement.
    • Things one could maybe try to grasp at, like the complexity or regularity of a language’s grammar, or the complexity/simplicity of its sound system, are just completely uncorrelated with prestige, and it’s unclear why we’d think one is better.
  • (I got the conservatism vs. reform idea from somewhere specific in the past; I remember reading it and thinking it was great, but I don’t remember where.)
  • Authority: the idea that there is some specific institution that has the legitimacy to determine correct usage.
    • E.g. with languages that have an official standard with a determining institution, like the Académie Française or the Icelandic Language Council. Or just respected authorities, like the Oxford English Dictionary – though the OED team regularly have to clarify that they only aim to describe English usage, not define what’s correct.
    • But I see no good reason why we should accept these as fundamentally legitimate.
    • There are some reasons people might give for why this is useful. E.g. that a shared common standard is helpful for teaching, or to facilitate communication in languages with a lot of dialectal diversity. I don’t find this all that compelling – English manages to have standard varieties that are globally comprehensible without any official authority. But in any case, prescriptivism is usually (at least implicitly) claimed as being correct in some deeper sense than just useful.
Instead, most linguistics-y people would say something like: All languages naturally change and develop over time. All languages through all of history have been the result of gradual language change; and primary drivers of that change include language contact/influence, and changes that make things “easier”/simpler in some sense (though that also can end up resulting in increased complexity in other ways). There’s just no reason to consider some language varieties or usages more legitimate than others.
Plus, prescriptivism is at least often tied to prejudice, classism, racism, and other such things which are widely considered bad.
Two asides
Aside the first: Interestingly, English prescriptivism seems to have started out as a reform movement in something like the C18th, but to have forgotten this and now prescriptivists tend to see it more in conservatism terms.1
  • This can be seen in various things that traditional English grammarians will push for, which were originally invented by reformers on the basis that they’d make English more like Latin, but were never common English usage at any point in the past.
  • Examples of this are the idea that one shouldn’t split infinitives, i.e. disapproving of “to boldly go”, preferring “to go boldly” or “boldly to go”; and that one shouldn’t end sentences with prepositions, e.g. disapproving of “that’s someone I really want to talk to” rather than “with whom I really want to speak”.
  • (You can tell that sentence-final prepositions are an old feature, because other Germanic languages like Norwegian and German, English’s sisters, do them too. Scandinavian languages like Norwegian do split infinitives too, but that’s not an old feature; two-word infinitives only arose after the Germanic languages split.)
Aside the second: the one, in my view, actually objective standard by which you can say something like that one usage is more correct than another, is whether native speakers use them or not. I’m still not sure I’d call a competent second-language speaker’s idiosyncratic usages “wrong”, but it’s reasonable for language learners to aim to emulate native speakers specifically. (And you can of course choose to try to learn/emulate a specific variety of your own language, but that’s a subjective choice.)
In which the author finally gets to the point
In my view, just because there’s no objective grounds for prescriptivism, doesn’t mean you’re not allowed to personally like or dislike some language usages!
I expect nearly everyone finds that some language features grate on their ears, while others sound particularly nice. And I think this is just… fine? You shouldn’t think that you have any fundamentally true basis for this, and you should probably try not to be outwardly judgey to people, especially in a way that embodies wider prejudice. But it’s fine to have personal linguistic likes and dislikes.
I personally have some dislikes of this kind, e.g. it mildly irks me when I see “if I would have done something” rather than “if I had done something”, or “different than” rather than “different to/from”. I just don’t think that that opinion is anything more than a personal, subjective dislike.
In short, I think that prescriptivism-like opinions should be mostly related to as aesthetic judgements.
Some extensions or connected opinions/facts:
  • Linguistic features that newly arose because of prescriptivism, are nonetheless now linguistic features that some people use, and are thus just as legitimate as any other.
    • For example, hypercorrections like saying “between you and I”. I.e. purity-minded reformers told people off for saying things like “you and me should go to the pub”, preferring “you and I should go”, e.g. on the basis that it’d work analogously in Latin or an earlier stage of English (which is true). But Latin / Old English would say the equivalent of “between you and me”, because there the “me” is in a different context, being the ~object of “between”. So “Between you and I” is a hypercorrection; people over-applying the imposed rule.
  • It’s fine to dislike a language usage even if it’s because you have some internalised prescriptivism. We all have our histories; most of our tastes are informed by our histories. If you dislike a style of music because it was socially disapproved of in your environment in the past, the disapproval was unfortunate, but you don’t have to seek out and listen to the music!
  • It’s fine for publications to have house styles, or for people to intentionally adjust their writing style, including to match a prescriptive ideal. Style and linguistic usages just do give different impressions to readers/listeners, and sometimes that’s something you want.
It’s fine – though it can be sad or reflect unfortunate social dynamics – for people’s speech to change, intentionally or unintentionally, including to match a prescriptive ideal. If I listen to recordings of myself in the past, it’s obvious to me that my voice got “posher” after I went to university in Oxford. The underlying social dynamics that cause this to happen are unfortunate, but I don’t think I did like a morally bad thing.2 In fact, social dynamics like this are just yet another longstanding and common driver of language change.
