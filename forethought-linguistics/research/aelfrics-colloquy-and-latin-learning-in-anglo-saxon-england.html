Abstract

This dissertation explores various aspects of Ælfric’s Colloquy. Unlike the other conversational teaching texts in the ‘colloquy tradition’, there is a great deal of contextual information available for study of the Colloquy. I therefore study the Colloquy’s use for language teaching in the classroom by reference to the manuscripts of the text and the glosses they contain, as well as the text’s reworkings by Ælfric Bata. At a literary level, I explore the wide variety of themes that can be found in the text – not only concerning language-teaching, but also teaching and language more generally, and the wide concept of moral wisdom that to Ælfric is the result of such teaching. This literary reading is grounded in the context of Ælfric’s wider body of works and his intellectual relationship to reformed English monastic culture.
Throughout my examination of the Colloquy I compare it to the other texts of the colloquy tradition within which it has previously been placed and find that in the matters I explore – linguistic teaching, literary themes and cohesion, use of humour – it differs greatly from them. Since there are also no demonstrable textual links to other colloquies, I therefore conclude by arguing that the Colloquy should not be seen as a part of that tradition at all. Finding that the text’s literary themes have very strong links to its author’s wider ideas, I propose an alternative interpretation as an innovative literary work for use in language teaching.

Introduction

‘Nos pueri rogamus te, magister, ut doceas nos loqui latialiter recte, quia idiote sumus et corrupte loquimur’.1 Ælfric’s Colloquy is structured as portraying a lesson, a discussion between a master and several students, and opens thus with a request by the student characters that is very specific: they wish to learn to speak Latin well. Ælfric here expresses his goal in writing, a goal that he shares with the colloquy writers that come before and after him. However, this opening already begins to make apparent the Colloquy’s literary sophistication: Ælfric, famed teacher, has placed his goal inside his own text; his text portrays teaching while simultaneously practicing it. This sophistication is expressed in the wide variety of connected themes and messages that can be read from the Colloquy, not only concerning the language teaching with which the text is primarily concerned, but also the relationship of this teaching to a more general moral wisdom.
Naturally, though, the Colloquy does not exist in isolation, and is only usefully and maximally appreciated in its multiple contexts. Previously, the text has been considered part of a colloquy tradition, comprising not only his pupil Ælfric Bata, but also the few other schoolroom dialogues that informed him. By comparison to this rather limited tradition, the Colloquy’s uniqueness is apparent not only in its literary cohesion and complexity, but also for the opportunity available to scholars to contextualise it: many of the ideas in the text can be paralleled in the wider thought and practice of its prolific author. I therefore study various aspects of the Colloquy with reference to the text and author’s multiple contexts: the glosses to the text and the manuscripts and reworkings of it allow exploration of the essential question of the text’s classroom use in language teaching, while Ælfric’s wider thought and his relationship to reformed English monastic intellectual culture inform and ground my readings of the text’s more literary aspects. My exploration of these ideas highlights the Colloquy’s uniqueness compared to the colloquy tradition, allowing me to conclude with a reconsideration of its place with respect to that tradition.

Reading the Colloquy

Before detailed consideration of the various possible areas of Ælfric’s teaching, before studying it together with the full complexity of the relevant historical contexts and texts for comparison, it is valuable to simply read the Colloquy as it is, for an initial understanding of the text, as well as for any features of its teaching that become apparent from such a reading alone.2 The Colloquy’s opening sentence, quoted above, as well as establishing the text’s purpose, immediately raises another question: such a request by the boys as an opening to a lesson inevitably seems rather idealised. ‘Uultis flagellari in discendo?’, the master soon asks; ‘Carius est nobis flagellari pro doctrina quam nescire’, the students reply, ‘sed scimus te mansuetum esse et nolle inferre plagas nobis, nisi cogaris a nobis’.3 This even seems rather wishful on a teacher’s part. The text provides plentiful opportunity for reading it as a portrayal of an idealised education.
‘Quid dicis tu, arator? Quomodo exerces opus tuum?’.4 The master thus soon launches his class into a session of role-play, the pupils inhabiting the roles they are given to answer his questions. Some students, including the first few – the ploughman, shepherd and cowherd – respond enthusiastically, telling of their daily work. Others, such as the hunter and fisherman coming next, seem a little less inclined to be talkative, so the teacher questions them more thoroughly, drawing them out to respond to his questions, leading the conversation when it needs to be led. (‘Uis capere aliquem cetum?’ ‘Nolo.’ ‘Quare?’.)5 As he moves on through the professions, the teacher turns the primary theme of his questioning away from the workers’ daily duties and instead towards their utility to their fellows. ‘Quomodo decipis aves?’, the birdcatcher was asked, but the shoemaker’s first question is more confrontational: ‘tu, sutor, quid operaris nobis utilitatis?’, as is the baker’s: ‘cui prodest ars tua, aut si sine te possimus uitam ducere?’.6 Through the teacher’s preparation, when the character of the adviser is introduced and asked the specific question ‘Que ars tibi uidetur inter istas prior esse?’, the students have become able to dispute the question independently among themselves.7 Not only does the teacher show sensitivity to the students’ needs, but the students are clearly portrayed as engaged; they participate intelligently in the lesson, and show noticeable improvement as the lesson goes on. Furthermore, I would argue that the teaching is portrayed also as entertaining – both the students’ and the teacher’s statements are often playful (‘O! O! Magnus labor!’) – contributing to the impression of the master’s teaching as engaging and sensitive.8
The main lesson over, the Colloquy continues as the students and their teacher discuss what they have learnt and their desires in learning. Why do they want to learn, the pupils are asked. ‘Quia nolumus esse sicut bruta animalia, quae nihil sciunt nisi herbam et aquam’ ‘Et quid uultis uos?’ ‘Uolumus esse sapientes’.9 It is no longer only linguistic fluency that the students have acquired: through their teacher’s efforts, they now strive for wisdom, a progression that Anderson saw as completed in the Colloquy’s final part, as the master, interrogating his students on their daily habits about the monastery, ‘guide[s] his pupils toward an expression of [that] spiritual wisdom in simple and practical terms’.10
It is not difficult to read from the Colloquy’s contents a commentary on teaching style, or on education generally. One might read an approval of giving space to students for originality, of paying attention to their needs and helping them achieve their goals, a message that learning need not preclude enjoyment, possibly a conception of learning as collaborative between teacher and student. One can also find an expression of the desired results of good teaching: a progression towards wisdom. Admittedly though, as is the peril of literary reading, one could just as easily be reluctant to impute any such subjective ideas to Ælfric’s text, leaving it instead as simply a language practice text, if a particularly engaging one.
However, above, I stated that a feature of special literary interest in Ælfric’s text is its dual relationship to teaching: it is not only a teaching text, a vehicle of teaching, but it simultaneously portrays teaching. So far I have considered not features of Ælfric’s teaching as attested in the nature of the text – that is, in the methods through which the text teaches its users – but rather in the features of the teaching that is portrayed in his text. Many of those features that have seemed to comment on methods of education are in this light self-fulfilling: if the actions of Ælfric’s teacher character seem to say, for example, that education in Latin conversation can be entertaining as well as edifying, this is confirmed by the same quality’s presence in Ælfric’s writing, his educational tool – assuming, of course, that Ælfric wishes his own text to be effective. As we move to consider the effects of the text itself, this dual relationship to teaching therefore makes it doubly secure to read from the text aspects of Ælfric’s own ideas on education.

Linguistic teaching and classroom practice

As well as Ælfric’s text, other Latin classroom colloquies survive that were known or written in Anglo-Saxon England – Ælfric’s own pupil Ælfric Bata produced two sets of colloquies.11 Puzzlingly, finding antecedents to Ælfric’s Colloquy in this tradition has not so far been successful; it does not seem that Ælfric made use of any other classroom colloquy when he wrote. However, from Bata’s work a tradition can be confidently traced back: he made significant use of the colloquies referred to as Retractata, themselves redacted, possibly by Bata himself, from the earlier Welsh or Cornish text De raris fabulis.12 Both De raris fabulis and Bata’s colloquies depend also on the Late Antique Greek-Latin collections the Hermeneumata pseudodositheana, which have been identified as the ultimate source of this Insular colloquy tradition.13 A cursory reading will show these various colloquies to have much in common: they are evidently texts for the practice of conversational Latin, consisting of conversations between various characters around the monastery.
To a modern reader coming to Ælfric’s work after these other colloquies, what first stands out in the Colloquy is perhaps an impression of coherence, of fluency. Bata’s work and De raris fabulis often present from the mouths of their characters lengthy vocabulary lists, or unsubtly showcase different morphological forms of the same word in quick succession, to an extent that can seem to impede their ostensibly conversational nature.14 Furthermore, while in Ælfric’s Colloquy, the events take place in one continuous narrative, Bata’s work and De raris fabulis are more fragmented, consisting of a succession of episodes of varying length, some of which are to the modern eye rather less entertaining than others. In this way, Ælfric’s Colloquy is unique among the other texts in the colloquy tradition. Indeed, whenever it has fallen to scholars to consider the unique qualities of Ælfric’s Colloquy and its linguistic pedagogy, I would argue that it is precisely this quality of seeming more readable to a modern audience that has ensured that the result has often not been detailed examination of his teaching methods, but rather empty statements of literary quality, often at the expense of Bata – to Garmonsway, for example, ‘the selectivity and economy of Abbot Ælfric’s famous [colloquy] is never matched again’, while ‘in [Bata’s] hands the colloquy runs to seed’.15
Hill considers the Colloquy’s linguistic teaching in somewhat more depth, writing:
[The text]’s variety has the pedagogical advantages of broadening the linguistic range, allowing for a high level of participation, and providing stimulating and challenging opportunities for changes of register, levels of discourse, and even dramatic characterisation of a modest kind. It is also an excellently constructed practice text in that the boys’ answers to the basic questions about the occupations naturally include lists inevitably arranged by subject, which thus exercise vocabulary in a way which is straightforwardly related to the Glossary, whilst at the same time this variety is carefully counterbalanced with a repetitious exercising of the common bases of Latin grammar, with dramatically justifiable changes of case, person tense and mood, embodied where possible in the sample vocabulary through which these features were abstractly presented in the Grammar.16
The Colloquy, she says, teaches grammar and vocabulary through practice and example, and she finds it effective in doing so.17 I entirely agree with this, and that the text’s literary features contribute significantly to that effectiveness, with the role-play structure offering opportunity for considerable variation of subject while neatly grounding the whole in the framework of the master’s lesson, never allowing the reader to forget the text’s educational nature. It is also certainly true that the text is very grammatically varied – for example, the forty-six words of the ploughman’s first speech contain a vocative, present and past participles, a subjunctive, a locative, and several ablatives absolute.18
However, Hill’s approach does not address the question of the Colloquy’s use in the classroom, leaving her assumptions unclear. A statement that the text ‘exercises vocabulary’, for example, is difficult to understand without this background – the text does not exercise vocabulary in isolation, but for its users, and the nature of this exercise depends on the nature of that use. I would argue that on the vital matter of classroom use of the text, and indeed the question of classroom practice of language teaching more generally, scholarship has made assumptions altogether too freely so far.19 It is therefore this matter that I primarily wish to address, both as the most appropriate direction from which to assess Ælfric’s teaching in the Colloquy, and as an end in itself.

The colloquies’ own evidence

The first category of evidence for the use of colloquies in the classroom – and classroom practice more widely – is that of the colloquies themselves. Although Ælfric Bata’s colloquies do not directly portray a lesson, several early scenes concern the schoolroom, such as when students argue over a lost book, or are encouraged to study in a lengthy speech.20 ‘Firmate acceptos uestros, ut properanter reddere ualeatis cras in primo mane’, the students are told.21 It is on the evidence of such statements that Lapidge summarises the ‘usual theory’ of general text-based medieval teaching: ‘each medieval student copied the set-text [from dictation] onto wax tablets’; they then memorised this acceptus in order to recite it the next day.22 Presumably the lesson would include both linguistic explanation of the text and wider interpretation.
This method of teaching may well have been used not just for literary texts, but for the colloquies themselves. However, it has been generally assumed, more or less explicitly, that colloquies, as implied by their structure, were used for conversational practice – that is, that to some degree they were performed, in parts.23 Of Bata’s colloquies, Gwara and Porter must bemoan that ‘there is no definite proof of their classroom performance’.24 With Ælfric, however, there is rather more explicit evidence for the use of performance in teaching. I have argued for the legitimacy of reading Ælfric’s ideas on education not only from the Colloquy’s teaching, but from the teaching portrayed in the text. Despite the many wider themes that may be read from the text, the lesson portrayed is, at least initially, presented as a linguistic one, and the master, asked for conversational teaching, immediately launches his students into the role-play that comprises the bulk of the text. The centrality of this performance to the conversational teaching portrayed in the text affirms the likelihood that such methods were a part of Ælfric’s classroom practice – although this does not necessarily imply that the Colloquy itself was used in this way.
Although detailed consideration of the linguistic teaching of Ælfric’s Colloquy has been rare, Porter has carried out such work on Bata’s colloquies, analysing their pedagogical methods from the perspective of modern language teaching research.25 He draws attention to Bata’s ‘apprecia[tion] of the divorce between meaning and structure – that a given meaning may be expressed with a number of different grammatical forms’, as attested in his use of ‘functional equivalents’, lists of phrases with similar meaning but different grammatical structure, enabling their acquisition without reference to English.26 Bata’s colloquies often feature direct lists of such equivalents (‘Quid quęris, puer mi, aut quid uis, quid cupis, aut quid aspicis, aut quid cogitas[...]?’),27 but Porter notes that Ælfric uses such tactics too, albeit in a more dispersed manner through his text, prominently with the questions he asks the workers: ‘Quid habes operis?’ ‘Quomodo exerces opus tuum?’ ‘Quid operaris tu?’ ‘Qualem artem scis tu?’;28 he hypothesises that Bata learnt such methods in Ælfric’s schoolroom.29 Such a feature would work for language acquisition however the text was used by the students: simply read, memorised, or performed in parts. However, the implications for classroom use come in Porter’s supposition that the lengthy sets of alternate vocabulary and phrases in Bata’s colloquies served not as fixed text but as ‘menus’ from which students could choose, adapting the colloquies during performance.30 As a strategy for teaching conversational language, this fluidity makes good sense: conversation is and must be fluid and adaptive. The sets of alternates which might otherwise seem to impede other colloquies’ conversational verisimilitude compared to Ælfric’s more natural-seeming composition may thus in fact improve their realism, as can be recognised by accepting that our initial impression of the static texts as they are written need not accurately represent the flexibility of their conversational classroom use.

Glosses, manuscripts and reworkings

The second body of evidence which might illuminate language teaching practice and thus the classroom use of colloquies comes from the manuscripts in which the learning of Anglo-Saxon schools is preserved, and especially in the glosses they often contain.
Monastic education was memory- and recitation-based to a very high degree, as was seen in the evidence of Bata’s colloquies. At an early stage in a novice’s education the most frequently used scriptural and liturgical texts were probably memorised well before a good standard of Latin was achieved, and it is widely accepted that much of students’ continuing language education was still achieved by working through texts – presumably of increasing difficulty – that were not only interpreted but also memorised.31 Glosses can present commentary to their text of various kinds – they can explain points of literary or allegorical interpretation, explain unfamiliar words by giving a translation or lexical equivalent, give aid to understanding passages’ syntax and morphology, or present metrical or prosodic information.32 Since text-based teaching presumably incorporated just such kinds of commentary and explanation by the teacher, glossing to texts that might have been worked through in the schoolroom has attracted particular interest since it may arguably preserve elements of classroom practice.
Lapidge studies glosses to a variety of manuscripts of poetic texts thought to have been used in Anglo-Saxon schoolrooms, and cautions that glosses often represent inherited commentary, copied from older sources just as much as the texts themselves are. 33 He argues that we should therefore be wary of considering such manuscripts ‘classbooks’ presenting evidence of contemporary classroom teaching.34 Wieland replies, seeking to establish criteria by which true examples of such classbooks may be identified, and indeed finds several manuscripts whose glosses he argues must represent classroom teaching.35 The debate over the status of manuscripts need not particularly concern us, given the undoubted educational nature of colloquies – and indeed the grammar texts with which they may travel – but the exploration of the relationship of glosses to classroom teaching is of the highest relevance.
Ælfric’s Colloquy survives in three manuscripts, which are all glossed. The Cotton (C) Colloquy is widely accepted to be closest to Ælfric’s original, although it already contains at least one interpolation, a variety of scribal transmission errors and a verbose epilogue that is almost certainly not authorial; it is accompanied by a continuous interlinear Old English translation.36 The Antwerp/London (R) Colloquy is itself preserved as marginal material alongside a copy of the Excerptiones de Prisciano, the Latin grammar of which Ælfric’s Grammar is a vernacular adaptation.37 This text is adapted, probably by Bata, adding additional details and extra vocabulary as well as making a variety of syntactic changes.38 The St John’s (J) Colloquy – preserved alongside Ælfric’s Grammar, Bata’s colloquies and Retractata – is extensively augmented by Bata with additional alternates and vocabulary, with lengthy lists taken directly from Ælfric’s Glossary; it has extensive lexical glosses into Old English in some sections.39
The purpose of the Old English gloss to the C Colloquy – which cannot be authorial since it extends over the unquestionably interpolated sections of the text – is not immediately clear.40 The Colloquy is a school-text, and it seems highly unlikely that any teacher of Latin would have had such poor skills as to need every word translated for him. It seems similarly unlikely that the gloss was included for students, since the scarcity of manuscripts means that students probably made minimal direct use of them, instead taking dictation onto wax tablets.41 In Wieland’s investigation of glossing as evidence for teaching, he finds several manuscripts which have glosses that provide morphological and syntactic information as well as lexical equivalents that ‘would appear elementary to anyone with a basic competence in reading Latin’.42 However, he finds glossing at such a simple level to be rarely comprehensive, instead covering only small sections of the text, leading to the paradox that any reader sufficiently unaccomplished as to need the glosses to read the text would therefore be unable to make much use of the manuscript, while a reader able to read it would find the glosses superfluous. He therefore concludes that the purpose of such glosses may be for teaching: they might remind the master to ask students questions on commonly-taught sections, or act as a key to allow him to respond to students’ queries without delay.43 Bayless studies a manuscript of the parsing grammar Beatus quid est and finds that its extensive and varied glosses, which interpret even extremely simple concepts – in places using them as starting points for rambling etymological exegesis – reflect ‘the kind of extemporaneous explanation that, although unrecorded, must have been common in the classroom’.44
Since the C Colloquy’s gloss is near-continuous throughout the text, Wieland’s argument for use in teaching is not fully applicable: one could see private use by a reader of little Latin as equally consistent with the evidence – however this may be rejected on the grounds of the Colloquy’s nature: it is highly unlikely any private reader would wish to consult it. The Cotton manuscript contains a large variety of texts in Latin and Old English and has been found difficult to classify – suggestions include that it is a teaching book, or a reference book of useful texts for a monastic community.45 However, it is notable that several texts in the manuscript have continuous Old English glosses.46 The Colloquy gloss is not idiomatic Old English, but instead closely follows the syntax of the Latin; it is also not entirely continuous, as towards the end of the text ‘some of the more frequent phrases stand unglossed as if, by then, they needed no further explanation’.47 It is therefore clear that it functions not as an independent translation, but as a key in order to better understand the Latin text. (Gwara and Porter hypothesise that this is true also of the other glosses in the manuscript, observing that the gloss to the Benedictine Rule – which is similarly ‘not quite continuous’ –48 is independent of the popular prose translation by Æthelwold.) 49 This is consistent with use of the manuscript by a teacher, who would not require the gloss to understand the text, but would use it in teaching.50 This also implies that the Colloquy was indeed being used as a teaching text at an elementary enough level that students would might need even the simplest sections translated – the Colloquy was indeed being used to teach Latin to beginners.
The glosses in the St John’s manuscript are more varied in character. This Colloquy is much expanded by Bata through the inclusion of large selections from Ælfric’s Glossary. Many of these new words are glossed in Old English, almost always with the Glossary’s equivalent.51 There are also at least nine scratched Old English glosses in the probably Ælfrician sections in common with the C-text – Lendinara believes these to derive from the continuous Cotton gloss, on the grounds that eight of the nine agree at least loosely with the Cotton word, and one, forlyþenes glossing naufragium, is a very rare term.52 To these words – naufragium, patior, iactura, uiuus, euadens, proficit, uescor, potus, senum, – can be added one more that I found in my inspection of the manuscript: witodlice glossing utique.53 Lendinara takes the nine glosses from Napier’s collection, however this does not claim to be exhaustive – there are likely to be further undocumented scratched glosses.54 The first five of the nine are clustered in one sentence (‘[...] et aliquando naufragium patior cum iactura omnium rerum mearum uix uius euadens’), and the sixth on the same page; utique is on the facing verso.55 Although the utique gloss is in the same predominantly Ælfrician section of the Colloquy, utique is an adverb added by Bata, common to the R and J texts but absent in the C text; there is therefore no corresponding word in the Cotton gloss from which it might be taken.56 Lendinara’s position that the nine words all originate in the Cotton gloss should therefore be reconsidered; the authority of forlyþenes can no longer necessarily extend to the other glosses, particularly uescor, potus and senum, distant in the text – especially since uescor is the one of the nine whose gloss appears entirely unrelated to the Cotton translation.57 These ten words are of varying difficulty, from the extremely basic utique to the specialised naufragium, and as scratched glosses they must represent the responses of users of the manuscript after it was written; I would suggest that these users were probably teachers, perhaps glossing words – or a sentence – that students asked about in class, or which they unexpectedly found difficult themselves. By contrast, the ink glosses, mostly taken from Ælfric’s Glossary interpreting the names of plants in a long consecutive list, probably serve more for direct vocabulary learning within a theme, similarly to in the Glossary itself.
Notably, both of these manuscripts are directly connected to Ælfric Bata: the St John’s by his colophons to each of his texts and to his reworking of Ælfric’s Colloquy, and the Cotton manuscript by the presence of Bata’s name on the original first page, as well as by its Canterbury provenance.58 Gwara and Porter have argued for the Antwerp/London manuscript also being connected to him, although this is shakier, with its basis in the inclusion of the R version which he likely redacted too, the origins of a small set of glosses, and that ‘whoever used the manuscript has access to all the language-teaching texts of Ælfric’s school [... and] to all the major sources of those texts’.59 The evidence of the glosses and manuscripts, therefore, can point almost exclusively to the practice of only this one classroom: Bata’s. (Indeed, I would generally stress that great care must be taken with evidence for classroom use if assuming any homogeneity of practice between teachers. We will naturally have the most insight into the classrooms of Ælfric and Bata, and the tone of their colloquies alone would imply these to have had rather different atmospheres.)
Porter, as seen above, argued for the prominence of active conversational teaching to Bata’s colloquies on the evidence of his use of ‘functional equivalents’ which would have their full effect only in performance; the text is therefore seen as a basis for more fluid classroom productions rather than as fixed in its words. However, other scholars have seen him differently: to Lendinara, ‘what predominates [in Bata’s reworking of Ælfric’s Colloquy] is not, as in Ælfric’s [original], the aim of teaching, but a desire to display his learning’.60 Garmonsway is even more derogatory, saying of Bata and other colloquy-writers not having the distinction of being Ælfric that they were only ‘ostensibly pedagogues’, ‘too anxious to display the riches of their word-hoard’.61 While I do not believe there is any reason to doubt Bata’s competence as a teacher, a valid point is raised: while appreciating the nature of Bata’s functional equivalents, his designed fluidity, does to a great extent explain the features of his texts that might at first reading seem to impede their conversational nature, the sheer extent of some of his vocabulary lists rather seems to go beyond the number of alternates that any method of classroom adaptation could reasonably require.
When Bata changes the C-text’s ‘quia uarie bestie morantur in siluis’ to J’s ‘quia uarie bestie et uermes atque animalia bona ac mala, munda et inmunda, morantur in siluis’, he is indeed giving his students more conversational options.62 When, however, he follows this by adding a list of sixty-eight animals directly from Ælfric’s Glossary, many of which could never have been seen in England’s woods, he arguably goes beyond this goal: Bata’s vocabulary lists may seem more to imply use for direct, more conventional, vocabulary learning. This is, of course, not incompatible with his conversational teaching, and it is entirely plausible that such words could be used in classroom conversational exercises, but nonetheless, different parts of his reworking seem to imply different direct uses in teaching. This is equally true of Bata’s own colloquies, which as well as swift-moving conversations also contain extremely long rhetorical speeches and collections of proverbs and Biblical quotations.63 Bata also writes the Colloquia difficiliora, recognised by Gwara and Porter as ‘where he steps away from oral, communicative Latin and toward Latin as the language of the written text’: although these are still colloquies, still framed as conversations, they prominently present the hermeneutic vocabulary that Ælfric avoids, a clearly literary phenomenon.64 Conversational teaching is therefore not the only kind of language teaching for which Bata shows a concern.
Nevertheless, significant parts of Bata’s colloquies, and Ælfric’s Colloquy as revised by Bata, were, we can argue with relative security based on the evidence of glosses and manuscripts, texts performed in the classroom as part of conversational teaching. A final piece of evidence for this comes in the Antwerp/London manuscript, which marks the Colloquy’s speakers with M (Magister), D (Discipulus), R (Responsio) and Int (Interrogatio) – it should be noted that occasionally the marking is incorrect; Hill believes that it was added by the scribe copying the text into this manuscript.65 For Ælfric’s Colloquy in its original conception, though, the question remains open.
While I have explored many of the implications of the Colloquy and its manuscripts, glosses and reworkings, and Ælfric Bata’s work for classroom practice of education, thus far I have only addressed education in conversational Latin as it was taught using these colloquies. The question remains of conversational teaching other than through these texts. Regardless of the use of the Colloquy after it was written, Garmonsway conjectured that adaptive performance is central to the Colloquy’s history: building on a suggestion by Lindsay he proposed that Ælfric’s general practice was to teach students vocabulary by ‘assign[ing] to each a rôle suitable to the [vocabulary] learned for the day’ and questioning them – ‘in the first stages he no doubt suggested specimen answers [...] but his aim was to encourage pupils to frame their own replies. [...] It may well be that the Colloquy contains some of the best of these replies, edited by Ælfric himself’.66 Naturally to some extent this must be true: it is impossible to imagine Latin conversation could ever have been taught without any free conversational practice, and the Colloquy’s portrayal of a conversational lesson could not be independent of Ælfric’s experience as a teacher. The colloquies known in Anglo-Saxon England are few – Ælfric’s, De raris fabulis, Retractata, the Colloquia hisperica; one could add the Hermeneumata pseudodositheana since they were used by Bata – and in only five manuscripts, three of which are linked to Ælfric and his student.67 While there is room for doubt as to how far the practical effects of the Benedictine reform’s ideals of monastic practice spread, it is nonetheless certain that Latin was spoken in monasteries more widely than could be accounted for if only these colloquies were used for its teaching: as Porter highlights, there is minimal evidence that colloquies were known outside Ælfric’s and Bata’s circles.68 One could compare their limited circulation against grammars, for example, surviving in hundreds of copies. In the absence of ubiquitous colloquy manuscripts, or of other texts for teaching conversation, the obvious conclusion is that the great majority of the Anglo-Saxons’ conversational Latin teaching took place orally, without great reference to written texts.

Humour and orthodoxy

As well as the Colloquy’s structural coherence and the absence of the functional equivalents that can seem so intrusive to a modern reader, a further very notable difference between Ælfric’s work and other classroom colloquies is the idealised, harmonious, respectful situation that he portrays, by comparison to the others’ sheer impropriety. ‘Audi, uxor pulcherrima! Ueni huc cito. Et osculare me et pone manus tuas circa collum meum’: the monks of De raris fabulis ask women to kiss them, they go into town to get beer.69 The impression given by Ælfric’s Bata’s colloquies is even more jarring, as the reader finds them utterly disrespectful to any idea of expected monastic standards of behaviour – his characters eat and drink to comic excess; they argue, throwing vicious insults at each other.70
Supremely relevant here is the context of Benedictine monasticism. Ælfric was a student of Æthelwold, one of the prime movers of the contemporary English monastic reform, and so is intimately connected with it – while I intend to consider Ælfric’s intellectual relationship to reform teaching below, the reform’s revival of strict Benedictine monastic practices is of great relevance to this discussion.71 Though no influence by an earlier colloquy tradition has been found for Ælfric’s text, there is nevertheless one very noticeable source – the Benedictine Rule. In its latter section, the Colloquy explores the students’ daily conduct: asked what he eats, for example, one replies, ‘Adhuc carnibus uescor, quia puer sum sub uirga degens’, referring to his status as still under the discipline applied to the young, and so not yet subject to the Rule’s most stringent dietary prescriptions.72 Anderson details the correspondences between the Colloquy and the Rule not only in the section on diet but throughout the text, finding that ‘the Benedictine Rule is […] the most important and pervasive influence on the Colloquy’.73 Ælfric’s text, despite its more than occasional playfulness, not only strictly adheres to proper monastic practice in its portrayal of young monks, but actively explores it.
While De raris fabulis naturally has no links to later Anglo-Saxon Benedictine practice, it is notable that such flagrant contempt for monastic discipline should be found in the works of Ælfric Bata, who was, after all, Ælfric’s pupil.74 In Ælfric’s Colloquy, a student, at the teacher’s question, defends his moderation in eating: ‘Uescor […] cum sobrietate, sicut decet monachum, non cum uoracitate, quia non sum gluto’.75 In Bata’s, by contrast, a student is required to decline repeatedly when an elder monk is forcing food and drink upon him: ‘non debes esse frugi’.76 For Ælfric’s student, ‘uinum non est potum puerorum’, but Bata’s will drain his vessel: ‘porrige mihi anaphum. Ego bibam usque ad profundum!’. 77 Dumitrescu discovers that, when ‘questionable’ material in Bata’s colloquies has its ultimate source in the pre-reform De raris fabulis, Bata in fact magnifies and draws attention to the sense of impropriety; her literary reading of the text finds that he uses such transgression of boundaries to help in his pedagogical aims by heightening drama.78 Related is Bata’s similarly prominent (and once again in places shockingly vivid) portrayal of physical violence in connection to punishment, whether realised or simply threatened or feared, which Dumitrescu similarly believes to be a part of his educational strategy.79
The factor linking Bata’s use of violence and transgression of proper monastic conduct is, of course, humour. Towards the end of his colloquies, there comes a lengthy speech where Bata seems to address his audience directly:
Sicut in hac sententia didicistis, […] iocus cum sapientiae loquelis et uerbis inmixtus est et sepe coniunctus. Ideo […] meatim disposui sermonem hunc uobis iuuenibus, sciens scilicet quosque pueros iugiter suatim loquentes adinuicem ludicra uerba sepius quam honorabilia et sapientiae apta, quia aetas talium semper trahit ad inrationabilem sermonem et ad frequens iocum […] illorum.80
As you’ve learned in this speech, […] joking is often mixed and joined with wise words and speech. For this reason I’ve […] arranged these speeches in my own way for you youths, knowing that boys speaking to each other in their own way more often say words that are playful than honourable or appropriate for wisdom, because their age always draws them to foolish speech and frequent joking.
Bata explicitly states himself to be using humour to maintain the attention of his audience. Does Ælfric do something similar? Ælfric’s restrained, harmonious colloquy certainly does not show Bata’s bold, transgressive brand of humour, but nevertheless I believe playfulness is a significant presence in the text – once again, present both in the internal teaching that Ælfric portrays, and coming through to the text’s reader.
When Ælfric’s student character playing the ploughman has given a rather lengthy account of his tasks in the day, the master replies ‘O! O! Magnus labor!’.81 A student, asked what he typically eats, gives a very varied list of foodstuffs, and the teacher’s reply is once again pithily amused: ‘Ualde edax es cum omnia manducas que tibi apponuntur’.82 Unlike Bata’s pupils, posting a watch for their teacher so that they can hide their foolery on his approach, Ælfric’s respect their teacher without undue fear; their relationship with him is portrayed as easygoing, to an extent appropriate to learning: ‘fuisti hodie in uenatione?’, the teacher asks the huntsman. ‘Non fui, quia dominicus dies est, sed heri fui in uenatione’.83 Assuming that the question was not a trap, the impression is one of some good-natured cheek by an observant student. ‘Non curamus de arte tua’, the cook is told, since ‘nos ipsi possumus coquere quae coquenda sunt, et assare quae assanda sunt’; made to defend his utility thus, he replies wittily, if in a manner somewhat divorced from grounded sense: ‘si ideo me expellitis, […] tunc eritis omnes coci, et nullus vestrum erit dominus’.84 Similarly, when the students have begun to debate among themselves the utility of their professions, they move a little past strictly academic points – ‘quid das nobis in officina tua nisi ferreas scintillas et sonitus tundentium malleorum?’ – but, as in the previous case, the teacher does not disapprove.85 His classroom is portrayed as foremost a place of learning, and the presence of humour as working towards this goal; so it is too, I believe, with Ælfric’s teaching text. It is so also with physical punishment – Ælfric’s students will, in the comment quoted above, happily accept being punished for the sake of learning, since they know their teacher to be kind – this is undoubtedly idealised, but similarly rather jocular in its incongruity.86
Dumitrescu reads Bata as using the transgression of expectations of proper behaviour to make his text memorable – and indeed in one passage as a signal of opposition to, breaking with, ‘traditional discourse of wisdom’.87 Bata’s text seems to see its brand of humour as opposing, incompatible with, authority, tradition, respect, propriety; it is these qualities of his humour that enable him to make the use of it that he does. Ælfric’s humour is entirely different. That he, orthodox, establishment teacher, will use only a much more temperate humour than his more freethinking student is unsurprising. However, it is precisely this quality of Ælfric’s humour that enables his different use of it: for Ælfric, humour, playfulness, is a proper part of his idealised view of teaching, of his harmonious view of the classroom and a teacher’s relationship with his students. While both Bata and Ælfric use humour for pedagogical effect, to keep their readers’ interest, for Bata this effect comes from a perception of that humour as subverting traditional education, but for Ælfric humour and teaching are entirely compatible. Bata is the ‘cool’ teacher, getting the children on his side with dirty jokes before they must trudge into another boring hour of grammar together; Ælfric is the classically good teacher, for whom the teaching and learning themselves can be entertaining.
As well as in the words of Ælfric’s characters, a reader of his Colloquy might find a further source of amusement in the characters themselves. At times, the students can seem rather like caricatures: the above-quoted boy playing the cook is good-naturedly creative, but does not quite manage to pull off his wit without losing a little of the sense of his reply. In their argument closing the role-play, the ironsmith, carpenter and advisor are all somewhat confrontational and defensive, but the advisor primarily evokes pomposity, with his moralising closing speech.88 Concerning Bata’s use of humour in his work, Dumitrescu identifies that ‘it is difficult to determine which parts of the Colloquies are intended to be foolish and which wise’.89 This is a difficulty that does not trouble Ælfric’s work in the slightest, as the nature of his humour is not such as to oppose teaching but to support it, such that the same sentence can work on multiple levels. ‘Magnum dampnum et uerecundia est homini nolle esse quod est et quod esse debet’, the advisor concludes.90 This statement is on one level the climax of this characterful student’s somewhat overzealous moralising, but it equally supports Ælfric’s social messages, concluding the students’ exploration of society – not to mention effectively showcasing irregular infinitives. Bata’s colloquies may well be ‘iocus […] cum sapientiae loquelis inmixtus’, but Ælfric’s play supports and is central to his teaching, and so to his students’ all-important progress towards sapientia.91

Language, morality and society

Society and community

The structure of the bulk of the Colloquy as a role-play, while certainly allowing for varied and engaging conversational practice, also results in an exploration of society as the students take on the roles of various workers. As the master questions the students, they recount the work that their character does, and certain themes rapidly emerge. Especially noticeable is what Colledge called ‘a theme [...] of profit or reward for a man’s labour’; one could extend this to include motivation for work, and obligations and relationships within society.92 The ploughman’s workload is great, and he connects it to his status: ‘Etiam, magnus labor est, quia non sum liber’; he is motivated by fear: ‘non est tam aspera hiems ut audeam latere domi pro timore domini mei’.93 The questions to the hunter first establish his identity, but then immediately his social place: ‘Venator sum’ ‘Cuius?’ ‘Regis’.94 His relationship to the king is then explored; he is freer, incentivised by reward:
Quid facis de tua uenatione? Ego do regi quicquid capio, quia sum uenator eius. Quid dat ipse tibi? Uestit me bene et pascit, aliquando dat mihi equum aut armillam, ut libentius artem meam exerceam.95
>
What do you do with your catch? I give the king whatever I catch, because I am his hunter. What does he give you? He clothes me well and feeds me; sometimes he gives me a horse or a bracelet so that I will exercise my skill more willingly.
The fisherman is more independent, selling his catch in the town, while the merchant is made to defend his practice of making profit: ‘volo vendere hic carius quam emi illic, ut aliquod lucrum mihi adquiram, unde me pascam et uxorem et filios’.96
There is also an interest in danger: ‘ualde audax fuisti’, the hunter is told, for killing a charging boar; the merchant tells of the peril he faces from shipwreck.97 The fisherman does not hunt whales, due to the danger involved, despite, as the master highlights, the higher prices such goods would command.98 A specific focus on utility soon emerges: the merchant is asked an open question at first, ‘Quid dicis tu?’, but replies strenuously defending his utility: ‘utilis sum regi et ducibus et divitibus et omni populo’; after this, the next three workers are immediately asked about their utility to society: ‘Quid operaris nobis utilitatis?’, ‘Quid nobis proficit ars tua?’, ‘Cui prodest ars tua?’.99 Ruffing sees methodical structure in the presentation of these themes: as the dialogue moves from primary producers to ‘secondary providers’, strain and danger in the professions decreases, while social freedom increases; the later workers must defend their utility precisely because it is not obvious.100
The introduction of the character of the adviser, the sapiens consiliarius, moves the Colloquy from serial interviews by the master into a debate, as the students, after the master’s initial question, ‘Que ars tibi uidetur inter istas prior esse?’, dispute the matter among themselves.101 The adviser himself is of no uncertain opinion: ‘mihi uidetur servitium Dei [...] primatum tenere’, to be followed by the ploughman ‘inter artes seculares’.102 After the ironsmith and carpenter dispute this conclusion, citing their own necessity to others, the adviser closes the discussion with a lengthy moral speech:
Sit pax et concordia inter uos, et prosit unusquisque alteri arte sua. [...] Hoc consilium do omnibus operariis ut unusquisque artem suam diligenter exerceat, quia qui artem suam dimiserit, ipse dimittatur ab arte. Siue sis sacerdos, siue monachus, seu laicus, seu miles, exerce temetipsum in hoc, et esto quod est, quia magnum dampnum et uerecundia est homini nolle esse quod est et quod esse debet.103
Let there be peace and harmony between us, and let each benefit every other by his skill. [...] I give all you workers this advice so that each will practice his art attentively, because they who abandon their skill will themselves be abandoned by it. Whether you are a priest, a monk, a layman, or a soldier, apply yourself to that, and be what you are, because it is a great loss and a shame for a man not to want to be what he is and should be.
This is not merely an appeal for peace, it is a defence, an explanation, a justification of the structure of society, of the varied differences between the professions and social positions that were explored in the preceding discussion. The stress is on interdependence and on community, on satisfaction with one’s place on the basis that all are necessary for that community. Nevertheless, the adviser’s initial opinion of the primacy of holy service is undiminished, being supported by the text’s only instance of direct scriptural quotation. Anderson sees in the Colloquy’s exploration of society ‘an expression of the Benedictine monastic ideal, [...] an economically self-sufficient community devoted to the service of God’.104
A reader seeking to see this as a universal message in the Colloquy’s structure, to see the society portrayed as a direct image of a monastic community, will be disappointed. Many of the workers featured are clearly secular, with the merchant being married, the fisherman selling his wares in the town, the hunter working for the king. I would indeed not necessarily argue that the Colloquy has any single overarching social or moral message, but nevertheless it is abundantly clear that themes beyond language-teaching are very apparent in the text.

Language and wisdom

Another matter beyond the linguistic that is explored in the Colloquy, one frequently arising in my discussion, has been the theme of wisdom. The Colloquy students initially seek purely linguistic instruction, ‘quia idiote sumus et corrupte loquimur’, but after the occupational role-play, during which a variety of social themes beyond the linguistic were explored, they have developed in their desires: ‘quid uultis vos?’ ‘Uolumus esse sapientes’.105 It is thus that the Colloquy’s structure can easily be read in terms of a progression towards that wisdom.106
After the students’ assertion that they now desire wisdom, however, the teacher interrogates them as to the nature of this wisdom:
Qua sapientia? Uultis esse uersipelles aut milleformes in mendaciis, astuti in loquelis, astuti, uersuti, bene loquentes et male cogitantes, dulcibus uerbis dediti, dolum intus alentes, sicut sepulchrum depicto mausoleo, intus plenum fetore?107
With what wisdom? Do you want to be deceitful and crafty in your lies, cunning in speech and generally, wily, good at making speeches and bad at thinking, given up to honeyed words, keeping trickery inside you, and like a tomb with a painted mausoleum, filled with stench inside?
The Colloquy’s schoolmaster usually confines himself only to asking the students brief questions, with the majority of the text consisting of their replies: discounting the non-authorial epilogue, this is the longest speech he gives. There is therefore clear emphasis on this vitriolic condemnation of empty rhetorical wisdom. The students reply, ‘Nolumus sic esse sapientes, quia non est sapiens, qui simulatione semet ipsum decipit’.108 One could even go so far as to suggest Ælfric is equating empty rhetoric with lies. The students’ next comment specifies the contrasting, correct form of wisdom:
Uolumus esse simplices sine hipochrisi, et sapientes ut declinemus a malo et faciamus bona. Adhuc tamen profundius nobiscum disputas, quam etas nostra capere possit; sed loquere nobis nostro more, non tam profunde.109
We want to be without guile or hypocrisy, wise so that we can avoid evil and do good things. But so far you have been speaking with us more deeply than we at our age can understand; speak instead to us at our level, not too deeply.
We now see that the purpose of this wisdom, and so of the learning the students receive in the Colloquy, is an explicitly moral one, hinting at a motivation for Ælfric’s teaching, linguistic or otherwise.
It is notable that these words are followed immediately by a concern for an appropriate level of discourse. Hill links this comment, an ‘overt recognition of levels of difficulty’, with the variation present in the text’s language – ‘formality, colloquial grumbles, vigorous protests, energetic debate’, showing that the Colloquy shows a concern for linguistic register.110 This is in fact the second statement concerning the level of debate in quick succession, as immediately before the comments on wisdom, the teacher asks his students how they found the role-play exercise: ‘Bene quidem nobis [placet], sed ualde profunde loqueris et ultra etatem nostrum protrahis sermonem. Sed loquere nobis iuxta nostrum intellectum, ut possimus intellegere que loqueris.’111 I would primarily read these comments as I have others, as showing a concern for attentiveness to students’ needs in learning as was found in our reading above, however an interest in linguistic register is undeniable.
Noticeably, these two statements sandwich the Colloquy’s discussion of wisdom. Teaching, and so the attainment of any wisdom through it, is now established as dependent on the language through which it is expressed, underlining the extent to which wisdom is deeply linked with language in the text. It is from language learning, we can read, that the students are able to proceed through their exploration of society to wisdom during the lesson, and the false wisdom that Ælfric denounces is characterised by the nature of the words through which it is expressed.

A wider view

Ælfric’s thoughts as expressed in the Colloquy concerning wisdom and learning, language and morality, his motivations for teaching, and even the structure of society, are a complex set of interacting ideas. Having explored them as far as can be done by reading the Colloquy itself, the next step is to turn for a better understanding to the wider context that is, unusually for a colloquy, available to us: Ælfric’s other works.
To begin with the Colloquy’s social ideas, at the most basic, the importance of Benedictine monastic practice to the text – a matter on which we have seen that Ælfric Bata, for example, had an entirely different attitude – is obviously an expression of Ælfric’s more general concern for such matters.112 The Colloquy’s social exploration – allowed by the text’s unique overarching structure as a single lesson within which the occupational role-play exercise can take place – can also be so paralleled. Lendinara notes that the interest in workers and society’s different roles can be found in Ælfric’s wider works, not only, for example, in his pastoral letters, where the industrious workers are ‘held up as examples to the clergy’, but also in the inclusion of the Colloquy’s profession-names in the Glossary and as examples in the Grammar (e.g. ‘suus faber est, his smið he is’).113 The adviser in his closing speech remarks: ‘siue sis sacerdos, siue monachus, seu laicus, seu miles’; Anderson identifies this as the laboratores, oratores, bellatores ‘three orders of society’ motif that Ælfric uses elsewhere, in various different kinds of writing.114 Powell, exploring Ælfric’s several uses of the motif, finds that his concern is often to stress the importance of the oratores to society, while simultaneously maintaining the importance of all three: ‘each of the three orders is understood to be fulfilling its own indispensable role’.115 The parallel to the social ideas in the Colloquy is entirely clear.
Turning to Ælfric’s thoughts on language, especially useful are his other works of linguistic pedagogy, the Grammar and Glossary. Ælfric addresses language teaching explicitly in his prefaces to the Grammar. Ælfric tells in the Old English preface that he wrote this English book of Latin grammar ‘forðan ðe stæfcræft is seo cæg ðe ðæra boca andgit unlicð’, to teach boys until they ‘to maran andgyte becumon’.116 While conversational Latin is the goal of the Colloquy’s teaching, the Grammar, we see, is concerned with the written language – apparently as part of the path to a more general understanding. ‘Iungum mannum gedafenað þæt hi leornion sumne wisdom [...] for ðan þe ðurh lare byð se geleafa gehealden’.117 This understanding, as might have been expected, is wisdom: throughout the preface, Ælfric establishes the necessity of his project in the Grammar, of his linguistic teaching, by presenting it as the foundation of learning more generally, of a religious wisdom that he links to the moral state of the nation:
Is nu for ði godes þeowum and mynstermannum georne to warnigenne, þæt seo halige lar on urum dagum ne acolige oððe ateorige, swa swa hit wæs gedon on Angelcynne nu for anum feawum gearum, swa þæt nan Englisc preost ne cuðe dihtan oððe asmeagean anne pistol on leden, oðþæt Dunstan arcebisceop and Aðelwold bisceop eft þa lare on munuclifum arærdon.118
God’s servants and monks must now earnestly beware, so that religious learning does not cool and fail, as was the case in England for several years, so that no English priest could compose or consider a Latin letter, until archbishop Dunstan and bishop Æthelwold raised up monastic learning again.
Linguistic knowledge is the basis of all learning, and so is universally important. This clearly parallels the progression towards wisdom seen in the Colloquy; that Ælfric expresses such a view here, in another text aimed at a monastic audience, strengthens the reading of language as the basis of the wisdom that the Colloquy students are portrayed as attaining.
The preface also points to another benefit of teaching for Ælfric: ‘hwanon sceolon cuman wise lareowas on Godes folce, buton hi on iugoðe leornion?’ – those he teaches may go on to teach others.119 In light of this, aspects of the teaching that is portrayed in the Colloquy as leading to the wisdom the students attain – the teacher’s responsiveness to his students’ needs and temperaments, for example, or the place of humour and playfulness in his classroom – might become doubly meaningful: Ælfric does not solely express ideas of good teaching as a part of portraying a successful lesson, but is actively teaching future teachers how to teach.120
The Latin preface to the Grammar is rather different in character, as Ælfric confines himself to more technical linguistic matters.121 At first, Ælfric’s primary concern is to defend his choice to produce a grammar written in English,122 but he then moves on to denounce the practice of speaking Latin with the Classical vowel quantities, which to him are fitting only for metrical verse.123 One could consider the Colloquy and Grammar to differ significantly in purpose, given that we saw the latter’s stated aim to be accessing written texts, while the former seems primarily concerned with conversation. However, the presence of this comment in the Grammar can be used to argue against there being for Ælfric any significant distinction between spoken and written language – further evidence can be found in that the example phrases found in the Grammar are very often conversational in character: ‘o pater mi, doce filium tuum’; ‘quam ob rem uenisti?’.124 This supports reading the Colloquy and the Grammar prefaces together concerning issues of wisdom and teaching – the two texts do not, to Ælfric, have fundamentally different purposes.
Central to Ælfric’s intellectual and teaching background is his own education at Æthelwold’s Winchester school: in the Latin preface, seeking to defend his use of Old English in the Grammar, he states: ‘Si alicui tamen displicuerit, nostram interpretationem dicat quomodo uult: nos contenti sumus, sicut didicimus in scola Aðelwoldi, uenerabilis praesulis, qui multos ad bonum imbuit’.125 It is widely recognised that Ælfric’s prefaces serve to establish his authority, and on this important matter of language education, it is the authority of Æthelwold, establishment reform figure, and of his school’s teaching that he invokes.126 Æthelwold’s school looms large in the record of late Anglo-Saxon scholarship, and there is plentiful evidence for its widespread intellectual, pedagogical and linguistic activity – Æthelwold’s biography by Wulfstan, for example, tells of his love for teaching and his use of the vernacular in explaining Latin. 127 Also associated with the school is the concept of Winchester vocabulary – a preference for specific Old English words over others especially in glossing and translating Latin.128 It is, of course, difficult to access which precise details of Ælfric’s methods derive from Winchester practice. His use of the vernacular in teaching clearly is of Winchester origins, but Ælfric also actively and independently considered his own language use, as seen in his revisions to manuscripts of his own work, regularising points of orthography and morphology as well as making lexical changes – which in fact have been found to often move away from Winchester linguistic norms.129 Clearly he was not a slave to his teachers’ thoughts, but nevertheless it is uncontroversial at least to consider Æthelwold’s school for the origin of most aspects of Ælfric’s pedagogical and linguistic thinking.130
The Latin preface also shows a concern with linguistic style: ‘scio multimodis uerba posse interpretari, sed ego simplicem interpretationem sequor fastidii uitandi causa’.131 Ælfric frequently addresses language in his prefaces, and a similar concern can be found elsewhere: in the preface to the second series of Catholic Homilies, he states he wished to translate ‘non garrula verbositate aut ignotis sermonibus, sed puris et apertis verbis linguę huius gentis’.132 He favours plain speech over, presumably, a more ornamented or pretentious style, both in order to engage his reader better and for the purposes of clarity. This is an obvious parallel to the concern seen above in the Colloquy for an appropriate level of discourse that will allow the students to understand and thereby benefit from their lesson. (‘Adhuc tamen profundius nobiscum disputas, quam etas nostra capere possit; sed loquere nobis nostro more, non tam profunde’.133) In the prefaces Ælfric is addressing Old English style and in the Colloquy Latin, but nevertheless the parallel is near-perfect: an inappropriately ostentatious style will impede teaching.
While this sentiment can be seen generally, there is a prominent specific possibility against which Ælfric’s comments should be read. Most every other Latin writer of his time – his teacher Æthelwold, his fellow Winchester alumni, even his secular patron Æthelweard – wrote in what has been called the ‘hermeneutic’ style of Latin.134 It is easy to see how this style, defined by its ostentatious, obscure vocabulary, is entirely contrary to the sentiment found in the Colloquy and Ælfric’s prefaces, and indeed Ælfric uniquely does not use it. An avoidance of complex vocabulary might be expected in an elementary text like the Colloquy, but Bata’s colloquies use the style, and, importantly, Ælfric avoids it throughout all of his Latin writings. His avoidance of the style is probably the most significant difference between his linguistic practice and that of his background – to Jones, the style ‘must have been viewed as a badge of reformed monastic scholarship’.135
It is true that Jones, believes that the simplicity of Ælfric’s Latin style has been overstated due to his personal fame, that the application of his comments in the prefaces is misguided since they concern the vernacular only; he concludes that Ælfric was not opposed to his contemporaries’ stylistic practice, but merely did not use it – possibly even because he was insufficiently confident at Latin composition.136 However I would follow instead the view of Stephenson, who stresses Ælfric’s methods of adaptation of his Latin sources, especially Wulfstan’s Vita of Æthelwold, finding that at all levels – allegory, syntax, vocabulary – he works towards transparency and clarity, seeking to shape his reader’s interpretation exclusively to the meaning he wishes to convey.137 She finds that Ælfric does indeed comprehensively remove hermeneutic vocabulary – although not necessarily complexity of syntax – in his sources, and concludes that ‘Ælfric’s avoidance of hermeneutic Latin arose [...] from very specific ideas about how language and narrative should function’.138 The radical departure from his intellectual background of Ælfric’s Latin style, in other words, is an expression – and, to us, an affirmation – of the sentiments regarding language that can be found in his prefaces and, I would argue, the Colloquy.139
In Hill’s words, to Ælfric, ‘grammar [...] underpinned orthodoxy, and was essential for a Christian society’.140 The Colloquy students, we could read, progress from their initial request for linguistic knowledge through an exploration of that society to finally appreciate the moral importance of wisdom – of a wisdom inextricably tied to the language through which it is expressed, a language that to serve that wisdom must be clear and plain. Ælfric’s Grammar is, obviously, a grammar of Latin; its purpose, one could say its entire purpose, is to teach morphology and syntax. However, Ælfric provides a preface to that text, in which is plainly presented a grander vision of purpose to his work, one that he wishes his reader to appreciate: language has a universal importance as ‘seo cæg’ to all learning, and thereby to all wisdom; to neglect linguistic knowledge is to neglect spiritual wellbeing, not merely in a reader, but in the nation.141 The Grammar and Colloquy, despite the one teaching written language and the other spoken, have much in common; the ‘puerulis tenellis’ and ‘iungum mannum’ to whom the Grammar is addressed are the same ones who would have used the Colloquy.142 There is no preface to the Colloquy in which Ælfric can shape his reader’s knowledge of that text’s purpose, however I would argue that, quite as prominently as the Grammar’s preface does explicitly, we have seen that the Colloquy’s text itself implicitly presents an argument to its reader for a wider motivation for linguistic teaching, a wider, a universal importance to language.
In writing his Colloquy, Ælfric was producing a text whose primary purpose was language teaching. However, we have seen that a wide variety of ideas – concerning language, wisdom, society, monastic practice – can not only be found in the text but also paralleled in the author’s wider work: in writing his language teaching text, Ælfric also allowed these wider pre-existing interests of his to feature. This can only serve, I think, to legitimise the supposition that other ideas that can be read from the Colloquy are not only interesting readings in their own right, but also reasonably reflect aspects of the author’s thought – and so, when these ideas concern teaching, aspects of that author’s, that teacher’s, classroom practice.

Reconsidering the Colloquy

Above I found that the manuscripts in which colloquy texts are preserved and evidence from glosses do show that Ælfric Bata’s colloquies, and Ælfric’s after it had passed to his successor, are likely to have been used in performance in the classroom with significant textual fluidity – particularly in the application of Porter’s ‘functional equivalents’, whose presence is a significant basis for this conclusion. These are ubiquitous not only in Bata’s colloquies, but also in the tradition that lies behind them – De raris fabulis and Retractata – and in his additions to Ælfric’s Colloquy. However, vocabulary lists and consecutive sets of grammatical alternates are absent from the C version of the Colloquy, that closest to Ælfric’s text.143 A question is therefore raised: was Ælfric’s original conception of his work for use in performance, as has generally been thought?
Gwara and Porter have observed that ‘a simple script without menus, as the Colloquy seems to be, would encourage memorisation, inhibiting natural expression and conflicting with the text’s pedagogical role’.144 I entirely agree; if Ælfric’s text was performed, we must conclude either that this was done with minimal fluidity, with the text fixed and the performance no more than a recitation split between different reciters, or perhaps that it was used as a basis for extemporisation that is not recorded in the written text. Gwara and Porter propose, given their belief that Bata learnt his methods in Ælfric’s classroom, that in Ælfric’s Colloquy ‘we are witnessing a text with some of its communicative elements removed’.145 However, there is no textual evidence for the Colloquy having been so revised, and I would suggest instead exploring the uses that the Colloquy as we have it would allow.
Ælfric Bata writes with access to a colloquy tradition in Retractata and its ultimate ancestor, the Hermeneumata, a tradition of which he makes great use in his own works.146 Bata equally uses his old teacher’s work – however, Ælfric himself has no demonstrable link to the wider colloquy tradition except through his text’s later use by Bata. To Lendinara, ‘the colloquies of Ælfric Bata and, to a lesser extent, that of Ælfric are modelled on the so-called Hermeneumata’.147 To Hall, ‘Ælfric’s Colloquy clearly grows out of this tradition since it adopts the same techniques to teach practical vocabulary and conversational fluency as De raris fabulis and Ælfric Bata’s colloquies, which likewise depict hypothetical scenes from a monastic classroom’.148 Hall also assembles various circumstantial pieces of evidence to defend Ælfric’s place within this tradition – the manuscript within which De raris fabulis survives is likely to have spent some time in Winchester; De raris fabulis is a Welsh or Cornish text and Ælfric may have learnt Latin from the Welsh teacher Iorwerth who features in two dialogue poems from Æthelwold’s Winchester school.149 However, none of this at all approaches proof, and I would say that we have no secure reason to think that Ælfric knew of the colloquy tradition within which he supposedly writes at all.150
Considered from the perspective of the colloquy tradition, the text is unusual, too, for its strong authorial figure: Ælfric is not merely a named author for the text; his prolific literary activity, his well-understood social and historical context, allow appreciation of his interests and ideas, especially concerning the teaching that is so central to his work, in a way that is utterly unparalleled among the other colloquies. The Colloquy’s extensive teaching beyond the purely linguistic is similarly remarkable. Above we could read a wide variety of messages concerning society, wisdom, morality, language and teaching from this very short text, a great many of which could be paralleled in his wider thinking. The literary cohesion of the Colloquy extends beyond the ideas contained in it; the overarching structure of the text as a single lesson, which I have argued is central to the text’s literary sophistication, is likewise different – the other attested colloquies are episodic.
The Colloquy is not Ælfric’s sole work of linguistic teaching; in this it sits alongside the Grammar and Glossary. Many have seen the Colloquy, Grammar and Glossary as fundamentally linked; to Hill they are ‘a carefully designed pedagogical triad’.151 The basis of this is primarily their shared vocabulary – Lendinara’s analysis makes it near-certain that the Glossary was used in the compilation of the Colloquy; similarly some of the Colloquy’s most frequent verbs are those used as examples in the Grammar.152 However, these links do not imply that Ælfric intended the three as a set. The Grammar and Glossary survive in fourteen and seven manuscripts respectively, from a variety of locations, and every copy of the Glossary accompanies the Grammar.153 The Colloquy, by contrast, is only attested in three manuscripts, all of which are associated with Bata, its later redactor and his pupil: it is entirely possible that Ælfric never circulated the text at all.154
Ælfric’s Colloquy, I argued above, has found widespread appeal among modern scholars because it seems much more readable, natural as a conversation to a modern eye; one could even characterise it as having become the archetypal example of the genre of colloquies, partly because of the absence of Bata’s lengthy vocabulary lists and alternate forms.155 However, in the light of considerations of the likely classroom use of Bata’s colloquies as adaptive, fluid conversational pieces, a use which those share with De raris fabulis and Retractata in their provision of Porter’s ‘functional equivalents’, it can be seen that this absence in Ælfric’s work would in fact inhibit the text’s effectiveness as a colloquy if its function were similar to those other texts. Given the lack of any demonstrable backwards link between Ælfric and that tradition, and given the wide variety of differences between his text and those securely within the colloquy tradition – the absence of built-in textual fluidity, the wide range of authorial interests beyond the linguistic present in the text – I would therefore argue that it is a mistake to see the Colloquy as existing within that tradition at all.
Porter, hypothesising that Bata learnt many of his methods under Ælfric, comes close to defining the difference between their texts as I would:
Ælfric's highly regarded acumen in educational matters is readily apparent [...]. True, he nowhere strings equivalent phrases together as alternatives, in the manner of Bata, but then Ælfric was foremost a writer, and his Colloquy is a literary work and a moral treatise as much as it is an educational text.156
Ælfric’s work is different to the other colloquies; this is correct. However, the step that needs to be taken is to recognise that, if by ‘colloquy’, one refers to the tradition of classroom colloquies for instruction in conversational Latin, a tradition to which Ælfric has no demonstrable link, then Ælfric’s Colloquy is not a colloquy.
How, then, would I characterise Ælfric’s work? ‘Nos pueri rogamus te, magister, ut doceas nos loqui latialiter recte, quia idiote sumus et corrupte loquimur’.157 The text at its opening expresses a purpose: its characters wish to gain linguistic skill. It is evident both from this statement and from literary reading that language and teaching are central concerns for the text. Equally prominent are the text’s wider themes, concerning society, morality and wisdom. I would certainly not consider the Colloquy to be primarily a moral teaching text – such messages must be read out of the text at a literary, metaphorical level, and it would be entirely against Ælfric’s established practice to directly teach on such matters in any manner other than with great clarity, specificity and reference to religious authority.158 In the Colloquy, though, and in Ælfric’s thought more widely, linguistic and moral learning are not at all presented as separate; language is a proper part of approaching wisdom, a wisdom which is explicitly moral in nature.
The solution is to characterise the Colloquy as primarily a literary production. It is a teaching text, certainly and entirely, but it is a static, written one; an interesting and varied one, produced to be entertaining as well as edifying, and written in sufficiently simple and accessible Latin prose that students would be able to read it earlier in their education than they could necessarily fruitfully approach ‘real’ texts. These elementary students would certainly improve their reading proficiency in Latin, but Ælfric, whose encompassing interest in teaching is attested in his extraordinarily wide array of writings, also placed his wider interests within the text. Given the prominent place of memorisation in medieval teaching, it is likely that the Colloquy was memorised. It may well have been performed in classrooms, it may not, but I would not assign to it the kind of fluidity that I believe characterises the colloquy tradition.
At the conclusion of his exploration of likely teaching manuscripts, Wieland attempts ‘a reconstruction of a typical lesson in the Anglo-Saxon classroom’:
After the dictation the teacher would explain the morphology and syntax of the text [...], would interpret the more difficult passages [...], would even provide lexical equivalents [...] to the words chosen by the poet and would dismiss the class with the instruction to memorize the passage.159
To step a little beyond the evidence, the Colloquy to me seems eminently suited to being the first text that elementary students would study in such a lesson.
One weakness of this characterisation might be that it removes any concord between our imagined conversation use of the text and the conversational lesson portrayed in it. However, I do not necessarily see this as catastrophic. We saw above that there does not seem to be for Ælfric a fundamental distinction between written and spoken language; the emphasis on teaching, on language as a basis for wisdom, is rather stronger than any specifically conversational nature of the text, especially given the ubiquity of the dialogue as a literary form.160 If the Colloquy were seen as a colloquy proper, then its central conceit would be in portraying a spontaneous conversation in its role play, when Ælfric’s real students, themselves performing the text, would be doing the very opposite: spouting memorised lines, not learning how to converse fluently. The Colloquy, then, can be read as an argument against just such a use for itself: it is through the master’s perceptive, unscripted, oral questioning that the Colloquy students are portrayed as learning and improving. When Ælfric, then, did himself teach conversation, this is how he would have done it: without a script, orally. I would still argue that the text presents historical evidence for conversational teaching methods, but that evidence is exclusively in the portrayed lesson, not the function of the text itself. The Colloquy is not a conversation text, it is a literary text – but one that, through the interaction of its dialogue form and its focus on teaching and learning, is a window into conversational teaching.
That the Colloquy has no links to the colloquy tradition that informs Bata does not mean that it exists independently of any textual pedagogical tradition at all. Garmonsway begins his tracing of the colloquy tradition from the more general classical and medieval use of dialogue texts for instruction.161 Anderson tantalisingly links the Colloquy’s role-play section with ‘a more general tradition of school debate over the relative merits of various crafts and callings’ that he believes to exist.162 To Hill, ‘Ælfric was working within the mainstream educational tradition’, since ‘the pedagogical use of predetermined dialogue was a well-established tradition in classical times and was exploited throughout the early Middle Ages’ – she furthermore suggests that the Winchester dialogue poems mentioned above ‘show that Ælfric came from an educational milieu which not only used Latin dialogues between master and pupil, but also produced its own elaborate versions of this scholastic genre’.163 Ælfric would certainly have known plentiful examples of the dialogue as a literary form when he chose to adopt that form for his innovative text. To identify a possible avenue of future work, an intriguing possibility for the origins of Ælfric’s interest in wisdom as the result of language teaching might be the Carolingian interest in wisdom and philosophy, as attested for example in Alcuin’s teaching dialogues.164

Conclusion and closing matter

Conclusion

Ælfric’s Colloquy can be approached from two directions. The first is to consider the text as it was used, taking as evidence the manuscripts in which it is preserved, the glosses with which users responded to it, the reworkings of its one known user, Ælfric Bata. Exploration from this direction has indeed found much of interest concerning Bata’s use of the Colloquy and needs and habits of teaching, which must take its place as a prominent part of the record of Anglo-Saxon classroom practice of linguistic pedagogy. However, as was seen particularly clearly in their use of humour and regard (or lack thereof) f0r orthodoxy and correctness, Ælfric and his student were very different teachers. The second approach is to consider the Colloquy as it was written, as a product of its author, Ælfric.
For all that I have argued for distancing the Colloquy from the colloquy tradition because of the extent to which its concerns are characteristic of its author’s thought, making it an innovative and independent text, it is equally true that that same innovative nature makes it unique, too, among Ælfric’s works. His Grammar is an adaptation of the Excerptiones de Prisciano, his homilies are formed from wide-ranging synthesis of patristic and medieval sources; it is through translation, adaptation, synthesis, re-presentation that Ælfric achieves teaching in his other works. However, the only textual influences found in the Colloquy so far are the Benedictine Rule and the Bible, texts entirely separate in purpose from its own. I would also stress the extent to which the Colloquy may be a chance survival: it is probably only due to Bata’s enthusiasm for it that it is preserved at all.
There are no prefaces to the Colloquy; Ælfric never ‘published’ it, never, as far as we know, spread it beyond his own schoolroom. In the Colloquy, then, we have a rare opportunity to see a different aspect of Ælfric the teacher: in this text that may well have been used only with his own pupils, he did not need to adopt his public persona. He did not need to drive home his every point with absolute clarity and painstaking reference to previous authority, did not need to apply his every scholarly and rhetorical weapon in the fight for his country’s moral progress. In the Colloquy, Ælfric was free to experiment, to create for his students an entertaining Latin practice text while simultaneously allowing himself to engage in literary play, making use of humour, incorporating his interests, resulting in a text that is as cohesive and varied on a literary level as it is on a linguistic.
