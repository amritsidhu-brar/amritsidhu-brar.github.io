How should we navigate explosive sound change?

Language change is already accelerating, and may soon outpace the ability of any single speaker to keep up.
If that happens, many new phonemes could arise in quick succession: new mergers and new splits; automated palatalisation and automated metathesis; superhuman analogy and superhuman hypercorrection.

Whistled languages
Mia Taylor
February 2026
If you can speak a language, you can usually also shout, sing, or whisper in it. In some languages, there’s another way of articulating words: whistling.
We take different modes of speech for granted—our brains can usually automatically translate the shouted or whispered version of a phrase into its spoken equivalent, and we usually pick up the shouted or whispered or sung mode of the language when we learn the spoken—so it’s hard to notice that something interesting is going on.
Take whispering. When you speak at a normal conversational volume, you push air through your vocal folds to vibrate them as you say the vowels or the “voiced” consonants. When you whisper, you clench your vocal folds to keep them from vibrating. This means that all of your whispered consonants are effectively “unvoiced.”

Linguistic prescriptivism and aesthetics
Amrit Sidhu-Brar
January 2026
Linguistic prescriptivism is the idea that some linguistic usages (e.g. words, grammar, dialects etc.) are correct/preferred, and others are wrong. If you ever hear one native speaker telling another that what they said was “incorrect grammar”, that’s prescriptivism.
This section just explains linguistic prescriptivism and why it doesn’t make much sense as it’s usually couched. Nothing particularly unconventional here.
People who study linguistics tend to roundly reject prescriptivism. The reason is that there’s just no good/objective basis for determining which usages are “correct”.

Ælfric’s Colloquy and Latin Learning in Anglo-Saxon England
Amrit Sidhu-Brar
May 2018
This dissertation explores various aspects of Ælfric’s Colloquy. Unlike the other conversational teaching texts in the ‘colloquy tradition’, there is a great deal of contextual information available for study of the Colloquy. I therefore study the Colloquy’s use for language teaching in the classroom by reference to the manuscripts of the text and the glosses they contain, as well as the text’s reworkings by Ælfric Bata. At a literary level, I explore the wide variety of themes that can be found in the text – not only concerning language-teaching, but also teaching and language more generally, and the wide concept of moral wisdom that to Ælfric is the result of such teaching. This literary reading is grounded in the context of Ælfric’s wider body of works and his intellectual relationship to reformed English monastic culture.
Throughout my examination of the Colloquy I compare it to the other texts of the colloquy tradition within which it has previously been placed and find that in the matters I explore – linguistic teaching, literary themes and cohesion, use of humour – it differs greatly from them. Since there are also no demonstrable textual links to other colloquies, I therefore conclude by arguing that the Colloquy should not be seen as a part of that tradition at all. Finding that the text’s literary themes have very strong links to its author’s wider ideas, I propose an alternative interpretation as an innovative literary work for use in language teaching.
‘Nos pueri rogamus te, magister, ut doceas nos loqui latialiter recte, quia idiote sumus et corrupte loquimur’. Ælfric’s Colloquy is structured as portraying a lesson, a discussion between a master and several students, and opens thus with a request by the student characters that is very specific: they wish to learn to speak Latin well. Ælfric here expresses his goal in writing, a goal that he shares with the colloquy writers that come before and after him. However, this opening already begins to make apparent the Colloquy’s literary sophistication: Ælfric, famed teacher, has placed his goal inside his own text; his text portrays teaching while simultaneously practicing it. This sophistication is expressed in the wide variety of connected themes and messages that can be read from the Colloquy, not only concerning the language teaching with which the text is primarily concerned, but also the relationship of this teaching to a more general moral wisdom.

English Easter Eggs
Mia Taylor
July 2026
Linguistics is one of the best things to be obsessed with. Seriously, think about it: you’ve done decades of full-time immersive fieldwork on at least one language, and overachiever that you are, you’ve memorized your entire dataset, plus you have a native speaker on call to badger anytime you want. Next time you’re struggling to fall asleep, try flipping through your dataset and looking for patterns.
You’ll first spot them on some easy examples. “Heal” + “th” = “health” and “true” + “th” = “truth.” Oh, so “th” must have been something you used to be able to add to an adjective or verb to derive a related noun. Then you search for more “-th” words—okay, “strength” is obviously “strong” + “th”, just as “death” is “die” + “th”—so apparently the vowels sometimes get a bit beaten up. Once you’ve noticed that, you can puzzle out some of the trickier cases. What’s up with “birth”? Oh, it’s from “bear” + “th”, giving birth is bearing a child. And mirth is the state of being merry, while something is dear when there’s a dearth of it. Filth is a bunch of foul things, while a broth is what’s been brewed. And so on and so on.
Every language has stuff like this. But, much like poetry, these patterns are most viscerally appreciated in a language that you know very well, so I’ll be sharing my favorites in English today. I’ve tried to pick patterns that are subtle, where you could speak English your whole life without noticing, but once you notice them, you start seeing them everywhere.

About

We are a small research nonprofit focused on how to navigate the transition to a world with fully reconstructed proto-languages.
Sound change might soon be much faster than the speakers undergoing it, quickly leading to rapid phonological restructuring. Even if every merger were aligned, we might face prestige-dialect autocracies, races to grab the last undescribed field sites, and conundrums about how to treat extinct minds, as well as opportunities to dramatically improve etymology and collective glossing.
Right now, we are thinking about:
  • How sound change and language contact might go
  • How to avoid academy-enabled coups
  • Which applications of the comparative method can most help with other challenges
  • What a great post-Proto-Indo-European future might look like.
