How should we navigate explosive language change?

Transformative AI could radically change the conditions under which languages are learned, used, transmitted and develop.
If that happens, many unfamiliar linguistic challenges could arise in quick succession: vast populations of digital speakers; languages evolving at superhuman speeds; costless translation and deliberate language design; new forms of communication unlike any used by humans today.
We are a nonprofit researching what we can do, now, to prepare.

Featured Research

Whistled languages
Mia Taylor
February 2026
Speech has already escaped the mouth before. Whistled registers demonstrate that linguistic systems can migrate across modalities without simply becoming new languages. Their existence helps separate features imposed by the human vocal tract from those that are more abstractly linguistic – a distinction likely to matter in futures where speakers can choose or redesign their channels of communication.

Linguistic prescriptivism and aesthetics
Amrit Sidhu-Brar
January 2026
Prescriptivism is often dismissed because neither tradition nor authority seems to confer a legitimate right to dictate usage. But if future speakers can modify linguistic systems deliberately, some form of choice becomes unavoidable. This paper argues that aesthetic preference over language is coherent even without objective correctness – a useful starting point for thinking about which changes we might want to preserve, accelerate, or reverse.

Ælfric’s Colloquy and Latin Learning in Anglo-Saxon England
Amrit Sidhu-Brar
May 2018
A text that both portrays and practices language teaching, of a language that had long had no native speakers but is of great cultural relevance, the Colloquy offers an interesting historical precedent for how post-AGI societies may look back on present-day languages and linguistic commentary.

English Easter Eggs
Mia Taylor
July 2026
Modern English contains layers of morphology, sound change and lexical history that almost no speaker consciously notices, yet which continue to be reproduced with high fidelity. These “Easter eggs” illustrate how much linguistic structure can survive through transmission without explicit understanding – and therefore how much historical contingency could persist even when future minds acquire language from packages rather than parents.

About

We are a small research nonprofit focused on how to navigate the linguistic challenges brought about by the intelligence explosion.
Digital minds, cognitive enhancement and radically new communication technologies could change many of the basic conditions that have shaped language throughout human history. We might see languages evolving at superhuman speeds, unprecedented linguistic fragmentation or lock-in, and entirely new forms of communication, as well as opportunities to dramatically increase linguistic diversity, expressiveness and beauty.
Right now, we’re working on:
  • Language diversity and costless translation. If perfect translation is effectively free, does language fragment into countless varieties, or converge around a few machine-set prestige lects?
  • Historical linguistics of modular language competence. Can inheritance and borrowing be distinguished when digital minds or uplifted humans can fork, merge and exchange their language skill?
  • Pragmatics of extreme-bandwidth metacommunication. What happens when an utterance can include its own representational system? When pragmatic meaning is carried not just by what is said, but by how this utterance’s grammatical system differs from the last?
  • Linguistic power concentration. What can be done, now, to avoid permanent linguistic lock-in by the Académie Française?
We also apply 10% of our researcher time to speculative avenues such as insect reproduction, kinship systems, and how humanity can navigate the transition to a world with advanced AI more broadly.
